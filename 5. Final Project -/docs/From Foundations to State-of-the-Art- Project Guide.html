03 What is image denoising? (from zero)

Start with intuition. The mathematics comes second.

The intuition

Take a photo in a dark room with your phone. The picture comes out grainy — speckled with random light and dark dots that were not in the real scene. Those dots are noise: random errors introduced by the camera sensor, not information about the world. Denoising is the task of removing those random dots while keeping everything real: edges, textures, faces, text.

Imagine that… someone whispers a sentence to you in a noisy café. You hear the whisper plus café noise. Your brain removes the café noise and recovers the sentence. You can do that because you know what sentences sound like — you have a prior, a built-in model of what is plausible. Every denoising algorithm ever built is an attempt to give a computer that same kind of prior about what real images look like.

The mathematics — one equation, and it never changes

The entire field rests on a single degradation model: a noisy observation y is the clean image x with noise v added on top. paper

y = x + v y = what the camera gave you · x = the clean image you want · v = the noise

The standard assumption is that v is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) with standard deviation σ. paper Unpacking that term one word at a time:

Additive

The noise is added to each pixel, not multiplied or blended. Pixel 200 becomes 200 + (something random).

White

Each pixel's noise is independent of its neighbours' — no spatial pattern, no correlation.

Gaussian

The random amounts follow a bell curve: small errors are common, huge errors are rare.

σ (sigma)

The width of that bell curve — the noise level. σ = 15 is mild grain; σ = 50 is severe. These are the three levels the DnCNN paper benchmarks. paper

Why this is genuinely hard: the problem is ill-posed

Your deck states this as the key insight of the whole field: denoising is ill-posed — infinitely many clean images could produce the same noisy observation. your deck

Imagine that… I tell you two numbers add up to 10 and ask you which two. 5+5? 3+7? 9.7+0.3? There are infinitely many answers. That is exactly the denoiser's situation: given y, any split into x + v is arithmetically valid. The algorithm must choose, and it chooses using a prior — an assumption about which x looks like a real photograph.

The classical Bayesian formulation your deck shows makes that choice explicit: your deck

x̂ = arg minx   ½‖y − x‖²   +   λ·Φ(x) left term = stay close to what the camera saw (data fidelity) · right term = look like a real image (the prior) · λ = how much you trust the prior

Every classical denoiser is a different guess at what Φ should be. DnCNN's radical move was to stop guessing Φ and let a neural network learn it implicitly from data. your deck

Why anyone cares

Your deck lists the applications: medical imaging (MRI, CT), satellite and remote-sensing imagery, surveillance and security, smartphone and consumer photography, and — importantly — preprocessing for higher-level vision tasks. your deck

That last one is the argument to lead with in your introduction. Denoising is rarely the final product; it is the cleanup step that makes segmentation, detection, and classification work at all. The DnCNN paper opens the same way, calling denoising an indispensable step in many practical applications. paper

05 Vocabulary you must own before you start

Every term you will meet, defined once, plainly. Open the ones you need.

Convolution & convolutional filter (kernel)

Intuitively: slide a small window (say 3×3) across the image. At every position, multiply the 9 pixels under the window by 9 fixed weights and add them up. That single number becomes the output pixel. Different weights detect different things — one set finds horizontal edges, another finds blobs, another blurs.

Technically: a linear, translation-invariant operator. The same 9 weights are reused at every position, which is why a CNN has far fewer parameters than a fully connected network and why it works regardless of where in the image a pattern appears.

DnCNN uses 3×3 filters throughout, with 64 feature maps per hidden layer. paper

Feature map

The output of one filter applied across the whole image — a new "image" where bright pixels mean "this filter's pattern is strongly present here". 64 filters produce 64 feature maps stacked like a deck of cards. The next layer's filters look at all 64 at once (hence 3×3×64).

ReLU — the nonlinearity

Intuitively: "keep positives, zero out negatives." Written max(0, ·). paper

Why it must exist: stacking two linear operations gives you another linear operation — 17 convolution layers with no nonlinearity between them would be mathematically equivalent to a single convolution layer. ReLU is what makes depth actually buy you anything.

Pooling — and why DnCNN removes it

Pooling shrinks an image (e.g. replace every 2×2 block with its maximum). In classification that is desirable — you want "is there a cat?", not "where exactly is each whisker?". In denoising it is catastrophic: you must output one clean pixel for every input pixel, so throwing away spatial resolution destroys the very thing you are producing.

DnCNN follows the VGG principle of 3×3 filters but removes all pooling layers. paper

Receptive field

Intuitively: how much of the original image one output pixel is allowed to "see". A single 3×3 layer sees a 3×3 window. Stack two, and the second layer's output depends on a 5×5 window. Stack many and the window grows steadily.

Because there is no pooling, the relationship is exact: a DnCNN of depth d has receptive field (2d+1)×(2d+1). paper Play with this in §15.

Why it matters: to know whether a dark pixel is noise or a real shadow, you need context. More noise → you need more context. The paper explicitly notes that high noise levels usually require a larger effective patch size. paper

Batch normalization (BN)

The problem it solves: as training updates the early layers, the distribution of numbers arriving at the later layers keeps shifting. Those later layers are chasing a moving target. This is called internal covariate shift. paper

The fix: before the nonlinearity in each layer, normalise the activations across the mini-batch, then apply a learned scale and shift. Only two parameters per activation are added, and they train by back-propagation. paper

Analogy: grading exams where the difficulty changes every week. BN converts raw scores into z-scores so downstream decisions stay meaningful regardless of that week's difficulty.

Benefits the paper lists: fast training, better performance, low sensitivity to initialization. paper

Residual learning

Intuitively: instead of asking the network "draw me the clean image", ask it "draw me only the dirt". Then subtract the dirt. Drawing dirt is a much smaller, simpler job than redrawing an entire photograph.

Technically: the network learns a residual mapping R(y) ≈ v, and the clean image is recovered as x = y − R(y). paper

Full treatment in §13 — this is the single most important idea in your paper.

PSNR — Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio
PSNR = 10 · log₁₀( MAX² / MSE )   dBMAX = maximum pixel value (255 for 8-bit) · MSE = mean squared error vs. ground truth

Higher is better. It is the standard metric for low-level vision. Because of the logarithm the scale is compressed: your deck notes that a 0.5 dB improvement is considered significant, and that PSNR is pixel-wise so it may not match visual perception. your deck

Calibration you should memorise for the defence: the DnCNN paper cites prior work finding that few methods can outperform BM3D by more than 0.3 dB on average — and DnCNN-S beats it by 0.6 dB at all three noise levels. paper

SSIM — Structural Similarity Index

A metric combining luminance, contrast and structure comparisons, on a range of [0, 1] where 1 means identical. It considers structural information rather than only pixel error, and correlates better with human visual perception than PSNR. your deck

Why report both: PSNR can be gamed by blurring (blur lowers MSE on noisy images); SSIM punishes the loss of structure that blurring causes. Reporting both is the honest thing to do — and the brief explicitly asks for comparative analysis using PSNR and SSIM. assignment

Blind vs. non-blind denoising

Non-blind: you tell the algorithm the noise level σ in advance. Convenient in a paper; useless in a phone camera.

Blind: the algorithm handles unknown σ on its own. Before DnCNN, the standard workaround was to estimate σ first and then load the matching model — which makes the final result hostage to the accuracy of that estimate. paper

DnCNN-B is trained across σ ∈ [0, 55] and needs no estimation step at all. paper

Discriminative vs. generative / prior-based methods

Prior-based (generative-ish): write down a mathematical model of what images look like, then solve an optimisation problem at test time for each image. Powerful, but the paper identifies two drawbacks: the test-stage optimisation is time-consuming, and the models are non-convex with several manually chosen parameters. paper

Discriminative: learn a direct input→output mapping from training pairs. No optimisation at test time — one forward pass. DnCNN is squarely in this camp.

Non-local self-similarity (NSS)

The observation that natural images repeat themselves: a brick in a wall looks like the other bricks; the stripes on Barbara's scarf look like each other. If you find many similar patches and average them, the noise (which is independent) cancels while the signal (which is shared) survives.

This is the engine behind BM3D and WNNM. paper It is also precisely the prior DnCNN lacks, which explains its two documented failure cases — see §19.

Epoch, mini-batch, SGD, learning rate
  • Mini-batch: a small group of training examples (DnCNN uses 128 patches) processed together before one weight update.
  • SGD: stochastic gradient descent — nudge every weight slightly in the direction that reduces the loss on this mini-batch.
  • Epoch: one full pass over the training set.
  • Learning rate: how big each nudge is. DnCNN decays it exponentially from 1e⁻¹ to 1e⁻⁴ across 50 epochs. your deck
  • Momentum / weight decay: momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e⁻⁴. your deck

What a "code deep-dive" actually looks like

The brief lists four things to explain. Here is a concrete question set that produces answers for each — use it as an interrogation checklist per repository. study aid

Structure

  • Where is the model class defined? What are its constructor arguments?
  • How is a "block" defined, and how many are stacked?
  • What does the forward pass return — the clean image or the residual?
  • Where is the config / options file, and what does it control?

Framework

  • PyTorch or TensorFlow? Which version is pinned?
  • Is nn.Module subclassed directly, or is there a registry/factory?
  • Is mixed precision / torch.compile / distributed training used?
  • How is the device (CPU/GPU) selected?

Data pipeline

  • What Dataset class is used, and what does __getitem__ return?
  • Where is noise added — offline on disk, or on-the-fly per sample?
  • What augmentations (crop, flip, rotate) and what patch size?
  • Batch size, number of workers, shuffling?

Pre-trained weights

  • Where are weights hosted, and how are they downloaded?
  • What is the checkpoint format, and how is it loaded (strict or not)?
  • Is there a Hugging Face model card? What does it claim?
  • How does inference handle images that are not a multiple of the network's stride?

Reading DnCNN as code (conceptual sketch)

The snippet below is written for this guide as a teaching illustration of the layer composition described in the paper. It is not copied from any repository and should not be presented as the official implementation. For Stage 2 you must read and cite the actual repository code. study aid

# Conceptual sketch of the layer pattern described in the paper.
import torch.nn as nn

class DnCNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, depth=17, channels=64, image_channels=1):
        super().__init__()
        layers = []

        # (i) first layer: Conv + ReLU, no BN
        layers += [nn.Conv2d(image_channels, channels, 3, padding=1, bias=True),
                   nn.ReLU(inplace=True)]

        # (ii) middle layers: Conv + BN + ReLU
        for _ in range(depth - 2):
            layers += [nn.Conv2d(channels, channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
                       nn.BatchNorm2d(channels),
                       nn.ReLU(inplace=True)]

        # (iii) last layer: Conv only
        layers += [nn.Conv2d(channels, image_channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False)]

        self.body = nn.Sequential(*layers)

    def forward(self, y):
        """Returns the RESIDUAL, not the clean image."""
        return self.body(y)          # v_hat

# clean image is recovered outside the network:
#   x_hat = y - model(y)

The one line to notice: forward returns the residual. This is the whole paper in a single design choice, and it is the first thing to check when you open any denoising repository in Stage 2 — does the forward pass return the image or the noise?

13 Residual learning — the central idea

If you understand only one thing from the paper, make it this.

Once intuitively

Imagine that… you are given a photograph with dust specks on it and asked to produce a clean copy. Two strategies exist.

Strategy A (direct): redraw the entire photograph from scratch, dust-free. You must reproduce every face, every leaf, every gradient — 99% of which was already perfect.

Strategy B (residual): draw a transparent overlay showing only where the dust is. Then subtract it. You never touch the 99% that was already correct.

Strategy B is obviously easier. That is residual learning.

Once technically

Discriminative models such as MLP and CSF learn a mapping F(y) = x that predicts the latent clean image. DnCNN instead trains a residual mapping R(y) ≈ v, giving x = y − R(y). paper

ℓ(Θ) = (1 / 2N) · Σi=1..N ‖ ℛ(yi; Θ) − (yi − xi) ‖²F Equation (1) in the paper — averaged mean squared error between the desired residual images and the estimated ones, over N noisy–clean training patch pairs {(yᵢ, xᵢ)}

Note carefully: the ground-truth target is (yᵢ − xᵢ), i.e. the actual noise. The loss never looks at the clean image directly.

The formal justification

The paper's argument is precise and worth quoting in your defence almost word for word. It rests on a result from ResNet: when the original mapping is more like an identity mapping, the residual mapping will be much easier to optimize. paper

Then the observation that closes the argument: the noisy observation y is much more like the latent clean image x than it is like the residual image v — especially when the noise level is low. Therefore F(y) would be closer to an identity mapping than R(y) is, and so the residual formulation is more suitable for image denoising. paper

Your deck states the same logic compactly: at low noise, y ≈ x, so learning F(y) ≈ identity is hard, while learning R(y) = v ≈ small perturbation is easy. your deck

Why "learning the identity is hard" is not obvious — and how to answer if a TA presses you. A stack of convolutions with ReLUs has no built-in way to express "copy the input unchanged"; it would have to arrange 17 layers of weights to conspire into an identity transform, which is a narrow and awkward point in parameter space. Meanwhile a network that outputs zero is trivially reachable — just drive the weights toward zero. Residual learning positions the "do nothing" solution at the easy point instead of the hard one.

Two distinctions people get wrong (worth pre-empting)

1 · DnCNN is not ResNet. The paper says so explicitly: unlike the residual network, which uses many residual units (identity shortcuts), DnCNN employs a single residual unit to predict the residual image. paper One global skip, not dozens of local ones.

2 · Residual prediction was not brand new — but residual prediction for denoising was. The paper notes the strategy had already been adopted in low-level vision problems such as single image super-resolution and colour demosaicking, but states that to the best of the authors' knowledge there is no work which directly predicts the residual image for denoising. paper Being precise about this distinction is exactly the kind of nuance a defence rewards.

14 The RL + BN synergy — why the pair beats either alone

The paper's most cited empirical finding, and a favourite defence question.

The claim, stated as a two-way street: the integration of residual learning and batch normalization can result in fast and stable training and better denoising performance, and the two benefit from each other. paper

Fig. E — The two directions of the synergy, both stated in Section III-C of the paper. paper

Direction 1 — residual learning benefits from BN

Straightforward: BN alleviates the internal covariate shift problem. The paper's Fig. 2 shows that residual learning without BN converges fast but is inferior to residual learning with BN. paper Your deck adds the concrete number: without BN, residual learning alone cannot match TNRD's 28.92 dB. your deck

Direction 2 — BN benefits from residual learning (the surprising one)

This is the non-obvious half. The paper states that without residual learning, batch normalization even has a certain adverse effect on convergence. paper

The mechanism it gives: each mini-batch is a small set (e.g. 128) of images. Without residual learning, the input intensity and convolutional features are correlated with their neighbours, and the distribution of layer inputs depends on the content of the images in that particular mini-batch. With residual learning, DnCNN implicitly removes the latent clean image in the hidden layers, which makes the inputs of each layer Gaussian-like distributed, less correlated, and less related to image content. So residual learning also helps BN reduce internal covariate shift. paper

Imagine that… BN is a thermostat that regulates a room by measuring the average temperature. If the room's contents change completely every few seconds — sometimes a furnace, sometimes an ice rink — the average is meaningless and the thermostat oscillates wildly. Residual learning strips out the "contents" (the image) and leaves only the noise, which has a stable, predictable, Gaussian distribution. Suddenly the thermostat's average is meaningful and it regulates beautifully.

The conclusion the authors draw

The paper is careful to attribute the win to the combination, not to the optimiser: both SGD and Adam enable the network with residual learning and batch normalization to have the best results — it is the integration of residual learning and batch normalization, rather than the optimization algorithm, that leads to the best denoising performance. paper

It also offers the deeper intuition for why denoising in particular: in Gaussian denoising, the residual image and batch normalization are both associated with the Gaussian distribution, so it is very likely they benefit from each other. paper

Likely defence question: "Would this synergy hold for a task where the residual is not Gaussian?" The honest, source-grounded answer: the paper's footnote explicitly guards against over-reading its own claim, noting this does not mean DnCNN cannot handle other general denoising tasks well — and DnCNN-3 goes on to prove it on super-resolution and JPEG deblocking, where the residual is clearly not AWGN. paper

How many slides is enough?

Why does DnCNN predict noise instead of the clean image? Explain it in one sentence.

Because the noisy image already looks almost exactly like the clean image, so a network predicting the clean image would have to learn something close to an identity mapping — which is hard — whereas predicting the small residual is easy. paper

The formal version: when the original mapping is more like an identity mapping, the residual mapping is much easier to optimize; y is much more like x than like v, especially at low noise. paper

Why does DnCNN lose on Barbara?

Barbara is dominated by repetitive structure (the striped scarf and trousers). Non-local methods exploit exactly that repetition; DnCNN's local receptive field cannot. The paper documents this and links it to the known finding that non-local methods win on regular, repetitive structures while discriminative methods win on irregular textures. paper DnCNN-S is 0.71 dB behind BM3D on Barbara at σ = 25. deck

Is a 0.6 dB PSNR gain actually a big deal?

Yes, in context. The paper cites prior findings that few methods outperform BM3D by more than 0.3 dB on average, and that the estimated achievable bound over BM3D is around 0.7 dB. paper A 0.6 dB gain therefore captures most of the theoretically available headroom. Always present the number with that calibration — "0.6 dB" alone means nothing to a listener.

What is the difference between DnCNN-S, DnCNN-B, CDnCNN-B and DnCNN-3?
  • DnCNN-S — "specific": depth 17, one model per known noise level.
  • DnCNN-B — "blind": depth 20, one model for σ ∈ [0, 55], no noise estimation needed.
  • CDnCNN-B — the colour version of DnCNN-B, trained on 432 colour BSD images.
  • DnCNN-3 — depth 20, one model for blind denoising + super-resolution + JPEG deblocking.

24 Key takeaways

① The project grades a story, not a bibliography

The evolutionary path from classical DIP to SOTA is named as a core requirement, and disconnected paper summaries are explicitly ruled out. assignment

② DnCNN is the middle of your story, not the whole of it

You need substantial content both before it (BM3D, WNNM, CSF, TNRD) and after it (2022+ attention and transformer methods).

③ Residual learning is the single idea to master

Predict the noise, not the image, because y already looks like x and learning an identity mapping is hard. paper

④ RL and BN are mutually reinforcing

BN fixes covariate shift for RL; RL makes layer inputs Gaussian-like and less content-dependent, which is exactly what BN needs. paper
